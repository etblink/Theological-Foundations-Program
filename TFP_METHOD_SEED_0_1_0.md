# TFP Method Seed 0.1.0

**Project:** Theological Foundations Program  
**Date:** 2026-10-05  
**Status:** provisional method extracted from the first bounded stress test (EMT)  
**Authority:** methodological seed; subordinate to TFP_PROGRAM_CHARTER_0_1_0.md

## 1. Purpose

TFP was founded to develop a theology-native way to compare theological claims without:

- disguising apologetics as research;
- importing inappropriate methods from unrelated disciplines;
- collapsing historical origin into theological truth;
- or treating interpretive plausibility as evidence.

The Environmental–Monumental–Theological Development Hypothesis (EMT) has now produced enough methodological evidence to extract a first provisional method.

This file is **not** a final constitution.

## 2. First rule: type the claim before evaluating it

Every claim should first be classified, where possible, as one or more of:

- textual;
- historical;
- archaeological / material;
- linguistic;
- interpretive;
- philosophical;
- doctrinal;
- empirical;
- experiential / practical;
- faith commitment.

A claim may straddle categories.

The method must then state which category is doing the evidential work.

## 3. Second rule: decompose long narratives into links

A compelling historical or theological story must not be adjudicated as one indivisible proposition.

Instead:

```text
claim A
→ transition 1
→ claim B
→ transition 2
→ claim C
```

Each node and arrow receives its own evidence review.

A failed arrow may break a proposed genealogy without falsifying every node.

## 4. Third rule: distinguish plausibility from attestation

For every historical-development claim, separate:

- physically possible;
- culturally plausible;
- archaeologically evidenced;
- textually attested;
- historically inferred;
- independently replicated;
- contradicted.

"Could have happened" is not "did happen."

Likewise, lack of direct evidence is not automatically evidence that nothing happened.

## 5. Continuity Ladder

TFP should use the following ladder when evaluating historical or doctrinal continuity.

### C0 — recurrence only
A similar motif, idea, form, or practice appears in more than one period.

### C1 — material continuity
Related artifacts, forms, symbols, or practices can be chronologically traced.

### C2 — carrier continuity
There is evidence of contact, population movement, institutional continuity, textual transmission, apprenticeship, trade, or another plausible carrier between periods.

### C3 — ritual / practice continuity
The form remains in a comparable sacred, communal, liturgical, legal, or institutional use.

### C4 — semantic continuity
The form performs demonstrably comparable conceptual work.

### C5 — named textual continuity
Texts explicitly name the deity, concept, doctrine, proposition, ritual, or interpretive relationship.

### C6 — genealogical continuity
There is enough evidence to argue that the later idea historically derives from the earlier one rather than being independently reinvented.

### C7 — doctrinal continuity
Where theology is concerned, the later doctrine can be shown to preserve or intentionally develop a specific earlier proposition strongly enough that the relation is not merely thematic.

No study may jump from C0/C1 directly to C6/C7.

## 6. Continuity of form is not continuity of meaning

The same object may perform different work in different periods.

Examples from EMT:

- boats move from practical transport to ritual procession to cosmic ferrying;
- cattle move from subsistence / status to ritual power, divine maternity, royal protection, and sky imagery;
- water moves from environmental necessity to seasonal ritual, royal renewal, cosmic water, libation, and afterlife regeneration.

The method must therefore ask:

> What survived?

and separately:

> What changed?

## 7. Refunctionalization

A recurring pattern found in EMT is **refunctionalization**:

> an inherited form survives while its social, ritual, political, or theological function changes.

This is distinct from:

- direct doctrinal preservation;
- independent invention;
- mere visual similarity.

TFP should treat refunctionalization as a first-class historical outcome.

## 8. Source-proximity rule

Where the question concerns an earlier period:

- later texts may illuminate;
- later texts may preserve;
- later texts may reinterpret;
- later texts may invent.

They cannot silently be projected backward.

A later named deity does not automatically identify an earlier anonymous image.

A later doctrine does not automatically reveal what an earlier ritual meant.

## 9. Independent-reinvention null

Whenever an environmental, psychological, social, or philosophical condition could naturally generate a concept more than once, independent reinvention must remain a live null hypothesis.

Examples:

- river communities independently associating floods with fertility;
- funerary cultures independently using boats for passage imagery;
- cattle-herding cultures independently sacralizing bovines;
- multiple traditions independently developing creator / order / chaos motifs.

Transmission requires evidence beyond intuitive resemblance.

## 10. Evidence convergence

Stronger historical claims should preferably draw from independent evidence classes, for example:

- archaeology;
- text;
- iconography;
- linguistics;
- chronology;
- environmental data;
- comparative material;
- institutional history.

Convergence is strongest when the sources are genuinely independent rather than repetitions of one interpretive tradition.

## 11. Alternative-hypothesis sources

Unconventional sources may be useful if they make a vague intuition more specific and testable.

TFP should not:

- accept a model because it is detailed;
- reject it merely because it is heterodox;
- use it to fill gaps that primary evidence does not fill.

Instead:

```text
ALTERNATIVE MODEL
→ explicit predictions
→ independent evidence test
→ preserve success / failure
```

EMT's reviews of Geoffrey Drumm, Graham Hancock, and Randall Carlson are examples of this rule.

## 12. Preregistration

Where a hypothesis is complex enough to drift during research, freeze it before evidence review.

Preserve:

- original claim;
- dependency structure;
- expected evidence;
- falsifiers;
- alternative explanations.

Derived hypotheses may be created later, but must not rewrite the original hypothesis.

## 13. Origins / development / truth separation

For theology, always distinguish:

### Origins
How did the idea arise?

### Development
How was it transmitted, transformed, institutionalized, or reinterpreted?

### Meaning
What did it mean to participants in a given context?

### Truth
Is the theological proposition true?

A natural historical origin does not by itself falsify a theological claim.

A theological claim being true does not make every historical account of its origin correct.

## 14. Equal-standard rule

Competing explanations should face comparable burdens.

For example:

- "traditional doctrine" is not exempt from source criticism;
- "naturalistic explanation" is not automatically favored because it sounds modern;
- "alternative archaeology" is not exempt from provenance and chronology;
- "skeptical reconstruction" must also explain the evidence it claims to replace.

## 15. Negative-result preservation

TFP should preserve:

- failed links;
- negative searches;
- unresolved evidence;
- chronology conflicts;
- attractive but unsupported interpretations.

A negative result can improve the map of the problem.

## 16. Scope discipline

A result must not expand beyond its evidence.

Examples:

```text
ONE FAILED PROPHECY INTERPRETATION
!=
RELIGION FALSE

ONE DOCUMENTED LATER DEVELOPMENT
!=
DOCTRINE INVENTED EX NIHILO

ONE EARLY PARALLEL
!=
DIRECT BORROWING

ONE FORMAL ANALOGY
!=
HISTORICAL DEPENDENCE
```

## 17. Preliminary adjudication vocabulary

Useful provisional dispositions include:

- `SUPPORTED`
- `PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED`
- `PLAUSIBLE_BUT_UNATTESTED`
- `NOT_ESTABLISHED`
- `EVIDENCE_AGAINST`
- `CONTRADICTED_WITHIN_SCOPE`
- `UNDERDETERMINED`

For historical continuity, pair these with C0–C7.

## 18. Method stress-test requirement

This seed should now be tested against domains unlike EMT.

EMT is archaeology-heavy.

A theology-native method must also survive:

- textual criticism;
- doctrinal comparison;
- philosophical argument;
- miracle / historical-event claims;
- scriptural authority;
- denominational disagreement.

If the method only works for archaeological genealogy, it is insufficient.
